Background

All NPS units in the Cumberland Piedmont Inventory and Monitoring Network (CUPN) take part in a monitoring protocol to track the presence and extent of ozone foliar injury on target plant species. Resource Briefs that summarize findings at each NPS unit in each monitoring year can be found on NPS DataStore under project reference code 2312827. Here, we show changes in the presence and rate of both suspected and confirmed ozone foliar injury at monitored sites across the monitoring period.

When data were collected, data collectors recorded individuals with what they determined in the field to be ozone damage. We subsequently refer to these observations as “suspected ozone foliar injury.” Field crews then collected a voucher specimen consisting of three leaves from three individuals for each species in a plot that had foliar injury. These voucher specimens were then sent to an expert for verification of the cause of foliar injury. That status (positive or negative for foliar injury) is then assigned to all individuals of that species in that plot that the field crew suspected had foliar injury). We refer to foliar injury data after confirmation by an expert as “confirmed ozone foliar injury.”

Exploratory figures

Ozone Exposure Index for Vegetation

The Ozone exposure index for vegetation is the “3-month maximum 12-hour W126 index that occurs during the growing season (March–September), measured in parts per million-hours (ppm-hrs). The W126 index is a biologically relevant measure that preferentially weights the higher ozone concentrations more likely to cause plant damage.” This definition and the ozone index values come from the NPS Air Quality in Parks website. NPS benchmarks, which are based on EPA standards from 2014, indicate that ozone exposure index values below 7.0 ppm-hrs are “good,” values from 7.0-13.0 are “fair,” and values >13.0 are “poor”

There are yearly ozone index data collected on site for some park units. For others, there is an interpolated average index for each year, which interpolates values from nearby stations and then averages them over the previous five years (inclusive of the “current” year). For parks with on-site data collection, we also calculated a five year average index to allow us to compare values across all parks in the sampling protocol.

Trend analysis

Question: Is the amount of ozone foliar injury at CUPN parks decreasing over the monitoring period?

Model attempt # 1:

  • The response variable is presence or absence of ozone foliar injury (there isn’t very much variation in severity of foliar injury – the data are very zero inflated– so we may need to use another structure for that model)
  • We will use a binomial generalized linear mixed model, which is suitable for a binary response variable
  • We will fit the model across all of the parks in order to maximize the amount of data available, and because we’re interested in the trend across all CUPN parks
  • we center the year variable, since the model will not converge with an un-transformed year variable
  • Because the data are not independent (plots are visited repeatedly, and the park unit determines the years when plots are visited), we’ll use a random intercept for plot nested within park.
  • We initially included a random slope for year according to the park intercept, but the resulting fit was singular due to the fact that the year random slope had low variance and was highly correlated with park. We also tried a random slope for year according to the nested random intercept for plot nested within park, but this model did not converge.
  • We include a random intercept for year as a factor (year_f) to account for year to year variation. Note that this is calculated as as.factor(centered year variable).
  • We also expect that occurrence of foliar injury is different for different species due to differences in susceptibility. We also think that the trend in foliar injury is likely different across species. To help account for this, we include a random intercept for species
  • I don’t think the observations are temporally autocorrelated, because the presence of foliar injury in a previous year does not affect the presence of foliar injury in a subsequent year (leaves re-grow annually).
  • The fact that plots aren’t sampled every year in most parks is likely not problematic, since the presence/absence of data in a given year isn’t related to ozone or other drivers (i.e. the data aren’t “Missing Not at Random”)
  • This analysis makes the assumption that parks are assigned to panels randomly, and not using some other variable that may be confounding with ozone quantity or one of the predictors in the models.

Look at patterns in simulated residuals

We already know that the random effect variables are driving patterns in the response (why we include them as random effects in the first place) so we expect to see patterns in the unconditional/marginal residuals) We use the DHARMa R package to simulate scaled residuals (“a scaled residual value of 0.5 means that half of the simulated data are higher than the observed rate, half of them lower.” – from the DHARMa vignette).
